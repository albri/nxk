**The best version does change by group.** Lead with the cheaper version for renters under 30, the weekly plan for parents, and today's price for people over 65. Parents split by income only in the thinner, less reliable way described below.

I read the three scans, which cost nothing to analyze. Each scan has 79 rows, and each row is one trait varied on its own. The measure is the share who say they'd "try it in the next month".

| Group | Today ($1.75/lb) | Cheaper ($1.25/lb) | Weekly | Lead with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Renters under 30 | ~11% | **~30–43%** | ~11–24% | Cheaper |
| Parents (1+ kids at home) | ~11% | ~20% | **~56–67%** | Weekly |
| Over 65 | **~11%** | ~7–8% | ~5–6% | Today's price |

**Renters under 30**
- Age 23 gives 43% for cheaper against 11% for both today's price and weekly. At 32, cheaper still leads, 28% to 24% for weekly.
- Renters without housing assistance give 30% for cheaper and 13% for weekly. Students give 59% for cheaper.
- Cheaper has to bring in at least 1.4 times the orders to match today's revenue per pound (1.75 ÷ 1.25). At about 4 times today's interest, it clears that easily.

**Parents**
- Interest in the weekly plan jumps from 9% with no kids to 56% with one child and about 67% with two or more. Cheaper only reaches about 20%.
- Income changes the picture somewhat, but only through the income scan taken on its own.
  - Below $75k, cheaper beats weekly: 47% to 4% under $25k, 30% to 6% at $25–50k, and 20% to 14% at $50–75k.
  - Above $75k, weekly wins: 21% to 16% at $75–100k, 27% to 11% at $100–150k, and 48% to 5% at $200k and up.
- Lead with weekly for parents at $100k and up. Between $75k and $100k the gap is small.
- Below $75k I can't tell you which wins, because the scan never combined kids with income. The kids effect (weekly ≈ 60%) and the low-income effect (weekly ≈ 4–6%) pull in opposite directions.

**Over 65**
- At age 74, today's price gets 11%, cheaper 7% and weekly 5%. Retirees show the same pattern (11%, 8%, 6%).
- Neither new version helps this group. Cutting the price would lower revenue per order and still not raise interest.

**How far to trust it**
- **Strong:** the under-30 and parent results. The gaps are 15 to 50 points, and irrelevant traits like personality moved results only about 1–2 points. That is a rough noise floor, since the files have no confidence intervals.
- **Moderate:** over 65. The gap is only 4–6 points, enough to rule out the new versions but not to say how much better today's price is.
- **Weak:** the parents-by-income split, for the reason above. Treat it as a hypothesis.
- **Limits that apply to everything:**
  - The scans are one pass each, with a single run per row, produced by a simulated-respondent model (`gemini-decision-1`) rather than real customers.
  - Stated intent to try it is not purchases, and it usually runs high.
  - "Renters under 30" and "parents" are inferred from single traits. The data has no measured "renter and under 30" or "parent and over $100k" groups.

If you want one cheap follow-up, a small scan of those combined groups (especially parents under $75k) would settle the weakest part. You asked for no new scans, so I haven't run anything.
